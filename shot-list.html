How to shoot them

  • Shoot wide, then crop. A web layout needs the same photo in landscape, square, and vertical. Leave room around the subject so it can be recropped without losing the shot.
  • Get people in frame. Photos with people consistently outperform photos of equipment alone. Empty gear looks like a product listing; people look like an event.
  • Golden hour or indoors. Harsh midday sun blows out the screen and creates hard shadows on the enclosure. Early evening is ideal, and it also matches when most events happen.
  • Shoot the screen in low light. The projection is invisible in bright daylight photos. Dusk or indoors is the only way to sell the visual quality.
  • Skip the filters. Heavy presets age fast and fight whatever palette the site ends up using. Straight-out-of-camera with light exposure correction is safer.
  • Highest resolution available. Downscaling is easy, upscaling isn't. A modern phone is completely fine — resolution matters less than light and framing.
  • Ask before posting faces. A quick verbal OK at the event avoids an awkward takedown request later, especially for weddings.

Before uploading

Resize and compress first. A photo straight off a phone can be 4–8 MB. Uploading a dozen of those makes the site noticeably slow on mobile, which hurts both the visitor experience and search ranking.

Resize the long edge to about 2000px and export as JPEG at ~80% quality. That typically lands around 200–400 KB with no visible loss. The hero photo already on the site went from 2 MB to 122 KB this way — roughly 16× smaller, and indistinguishable on screen.

Name files descriptively before uploading — golf-simulator-wedding-raleigh.jpg, not IMG_4821.jpg. Filenames and alt text are read by Google Images, which is a real source of traffic for a visual service business.
